BOOK ONE — THE MAKING

In which there is nothing, and then a great deal of it

I. Before

There was nothing, and no one to notice it.

Gröna Elden stood in it anyway. Grey hair, dark coat, spectacles catching a light that had not been made yet. He looked at the emptiness for a long time and found nothing in it worth keeping.

"I will make a thing," He said, to no one, because there was no one. "And then I will make another thing out of that."

He raised one hand, and fire came out of it.

That is the first thing I know, and I was not there. I have it from those who were, and they were mostly the fire. They do not agree about the rest.

The light spread and filled the emptiness, and He called it the First Radiance, and told it not to outshine Him, which tells you what He was like. Night objected to being separated from the light. He gave it hours and it obeyed, which tells you what everything else was like.

He made the stars and said there should be many, "that the night may not be entirely useless." I have kept that sentence for a long time. It is the only joke in the book, and He did not know it was one.

He made the sun and set its course. He made the moon and put it on the other side, so that neither should have to look at the other.

He made the winds and named them for the four directions, and told them to keep moving. They have not stopped. I have listened to them for a great many years and I do not believe they are going anywhere in particular.

II. The Beasts

He gathered the soil of the eastern plains and made trees, and gave them roots so they would stay, and branches so they could reach, and fruit so that whatever came later would have something to eat.

He made the grasses, and the flowers, and the small things that live in the grass and are not seen.

He made the fish and gave them fins. He made the birds and gave them wings, and they went up at once, which He had not expected.

He made the beasts of the land, and among them He made the donkey.

The donkey looked at its Maker, opened its mouth, and produced a sound that disturbed the heavens.

Gröna Elden turned toward it. "What is this noise?"

The donkey made the noise again.

"Very well," He said. "Thou shalt remain."

And the donkey kicked the ground, and a depression appeared beneath its feet, and that is how the first valley was made — not by design, but by an animal disagreeing with the arrangement.

He kept the donkey. I asked Him once why, and He said, "It is the only one that answers back." I did not ask again.

He made the cattle, the horses, the swine, and the things that live under the earth and are never grateful.

Then He looked at all of it and saw that none of it looked like Him. This troubled Him more than He would admit.

III. The People

He took the dust again and made a man, and breathed into him, and the man opened his eyes and asked, "What am I?"

"A man," said Gröna Elden. "Thou hast been made upon the earth."

"And who art Thou?"

"I am Gröna Elden."

The man considered this, and then asked a third question, and then a fourth, and it became clear to the Maker that He had made a creature that could ask questions and had not made one that could stop.

He did not undo it. He has never undone anything He has made, which is either mercy or stubbornness, and I have never been able to decide which.

The people multiplied. They built beside the water. They named the stars themselves, badly. They buried their dead and then dug them up to look at them again.

And He watched, and said very little, and began to understand what He had done.

BOOK TWO — THE PIG

In which a creature is made incorrectly, and decides to do something about it

I. The Creature

I was born in the eastern lands, where the soil is dark and the trees grow tall, and I was born wrong.

My body was the body of a man. My head was the head of a swine. My snout was broad, my ears stood above my head, and there was no arrangement of my face that a mother could call handsome.

I could walk upright. I could think. That was the difficulty. A stupid pig-man is a joke. A clever one is an accusation.

The children made the noise at me. The elders said I had been fashioned incorrectly, and said it in the tone people use when they mean that God has made a mistake and they are being polite about it.

I told them that the One who made me had not made a mistake.

I had no evidence for this. I want that noted. I had no evidence, and I said it anyway, and they hated me for it more than for my face.

So I left their company and walked under the stars alone, and I listened, and I heard what everyone hears: rumours of the Great One, whose face had been cut into the oldest stones.

I studied those stones for years. I learned the line of the jaw and the shape of the spectacles. I could have drawn Him from memory before I ever saw Him.

That is not the same as knowing someone. I did not know that yet.

II. The Mountain

I climbed the highest mountain in the eastern lands. Three days up, one day without water, and on the fourth morning I reached the top.

There was no one there.

I want to be exact about this, because the book has been read aloud in places I have never been, and the version they tell is not the version I wrote. There was no old man on a stone. There was wind, and a great deal of sky, and the summit was small and cold and had nothing on it at all.

I waited. I argued with the sky. I said, "I have come a long way." The sky did what sky does.

I have thought since that perhaps He was somewhere else that morning, doing something small — mending a thing, watching a bird — and could not be troubled. I have thought that perhaps He looked at me and decided not to come. Both are worse than being refused, and I have not settled which one I believe.

On the fifth day I came down.

III. The Lie

I told them I had seen Him.

I described the hair, grey. The spectacles, broad. The moustache, which no man could fashion without considerable effort. I described the voice, which was low and unhurried, and the hands, which were old.

They believed me. Of course they believed me. I had spent years learning the stones, and I gave them back their own god with a face they recognised, and they wept, and I was, for the first time in my life, somebody.

They asked what He had said.

I had not prepared that part. So I gave them what they wanted: that He had chosen them, that He had remembered them, that they should build a house and cut His face above the door.

They built it in a season. I stood in the doorway of a house I had invented, in front of a god I had not met, and I felt the warm particular pleasure of being believed.

I have written this book to explain that pleasure. I have not yet succeeded.

BOOK THREE — THE CALLING

In which someone else is called, and the record is given to the wrong man

I. At the Well

ChooChooCReW was a man whose countenance was pleasant and whose speech came easily, and the people were glad when he came among them, and no one had ever said he was made incorrectly.

He was at the well on the eastern road, drawing water, when Gröna Elden sat down on the wall beside him.

"Thou," said the Maker.

"Me?" said ChooChooCReW.

"Bring them. Whichever ones come."

"Bring them where?"

"Here is sufficient."

And that was the calling. Four sentences at a well, before the sun was high. No mountain, no fire, no trembling of the ground. I have written it down exactly, and I hate it.

He told the people. They came. I came.

The Maker looked at the assembly, and then at ChooChooCReW, and did not look at me.

And then He did the thing I have never forgiven. He turned to me and said, "Thou hast the steadiest hand among them. Write it down."

So the record is mine. He gave it to me because I could hold a pen, and He had not called me, and He has never once asked what I wrote.

II. The First Meeting

The people gathered in the house with the face above the door, and ChooChooCReW spoke, and they believed him, and I wrote.

He told them the Maker had appeared at the well. He told them the Maker had said "here is sufficient." He did not embellish. He did not need to.

And I sat at the side with the pen, and made it sound like a mountain.

The donkey came in halfway through and stood at the back, and did not join, and left before the closing. I have always respected that animal.

BOOK FOUR — THE TEACHINGS

Which emptied the church

I. On What You Were

The people asked, "Where were we before we were born?"

And He said: "Nowhere. There was no room in which thou waitedst, and no hour in which thou wast expected. Thou wast not kept anywhere. Thou wast not known. Thou wast nothing, and then thou wast this."

They did not like it. They had been told, by me, that they had been waiting.

"Do not say thou wast known before thou wast made," He said. "It is a pleasant thought and it is not true. I did not miss thee before thou camest. There was nothing of thee to miss."

II. On What You May Become

And they asked the other question, the one they had come for. "May we become as Thou art?"

And He said: "No."

They waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it.

"There is no ladder," He said at last. "There is a floor, and there is Me, and the floor is good. Thou wilt not climb it. Thou wilt stand on it, and be a man, and die, and that is the whole of the arrangement."

"But we were told —"

"Thou wast told by a man with a pen."

I was sitting at the side. I did not look up.

III. On Families

And the women asked, "What of our dead? What of our children? Shall we have them again?"

And He said: "A family is a room thou art in for a while. The door closes. Do not build a doctrine on the hope that it does not."

They said that this was unbearable.

"It is not unbearable," He said. "It is only unbearable to be told. Thou hast been living with it thy whole life and calling it Tuesday."

And He said: "The dead are not waiting for thy paperwork."

IV. On Prayer, Prophets, and Buildings

And they asked how they should pray.

"Thou mayest speak to Me," He said. "I am not listening, but speak. It is good for the throat."

They asked whether He would answer.

"I have answered everything already. It was short. Thou wast not paying attention."

They asked whether there would be more prophets after ChooChooCReW.

"There will be no more prophets," He said. "I have said what I have said. Any man who comes to thee after this saying 'thus saith the Lord' is quoting himself."

And they asked about the house with the face above the door, and whether they should build more.

"Every house built for Me is a house I must now avoid," He said. "I am not a thing that lives in a room. Take the face down. It is a good likeness and it is not Me."

V. On Money, Missions, and the Body

And the keepers asked what they should give.

"Nothing," He said. "Give nothing to a man who asks for money in My name. He has already left Me; the money is the proof."

And they asked whether they should go unto the nations and tell them.

"Do not," He said. "It is better they do not know. Thou wilt not be doing them a kindness. Thou wilt be giving them a book, and then they will have to carry it."

And one of them, being devout, said, "But our bodies are temples."

"Thy body is a coat," said Gröna Elden. "Wear it out. Do not call it a temple; temples are the buildings I have just told thee to stop making."

And He said: "Eat what thou wilt and drink what thou wilt. I do not require thy food, and I have never been improved by thy drink."

And they asked what came after, and whether there were degrees of it, and which was the highest.

"There is one place after this," He said, "and it is adequate. Do not ask Me to rank thee. I have never ranked anyone. It is the ranking that has been done to thee, not by Me."

BOOK FIVE — THE LEAVING

In which everyone goes, and the last one goes last

I. The First to Go

They did not argue. That is what I remember. They were polite about it.

The wealthy left first, when they understood that there would be no accounting of their money and no reward for it. Then the ones who had come to become gods, when they understood that the gap was the point. Then the families — not the ones with children, the ones who had buried somebody — when they understood that the door closes and there is no paperwork.

They said things like "I have to see to the field." They shook hands. One man thanked the Maker for His honesty, which I thought was the cruellest thing said that year.

Within four seasons the house was empty except for myself, ChooChooCReW, and the donkey, which had never joined and now had no reason to leave.

II. The Last Question

ChooChooCReW stayed longest because he was the only one who had been called, and he kept thinking the calling must mean something.

He asked, "Is there anything after?"

"There is something," said Gröna Elden. "It is not what thou wast promised."

"Then I will go and see."

"Thou wilt not be able to tell Me about it."

"I know," said ChooChooCReW. "I would like to see it anyway."

And he went. Not in anger. He shook the Maker's hand, and he was polite, and he did not look at me, and I have not seen him since.

He was the best of them. I want that written down, and I want it written that he did not stay, and that being the best was not enough, and that I have thought about this more than I have thought about anything.

III. The Scribe's Request

And I was alone with Him, and I asked the question I had climbed a mountain to ask.

"Call me."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because thou wouldst write it down as a calling, and it was not one."

"I have served Thee. I have kept Thy record. I have believed in Thee longer than anyone."

"Thou hast believed in a face on a stone," He said. "It is a good face. It is not Me."

And I asked the other thing, the thing I have never said aloud before this page, and He answered it as He answers everything: plainly, and without cruelty, which is worse.

"I made thee," He said. "That is not the same as loving thee, and I will not pretend it is."

So I took the record, and I left, and on the way out I set fire to the house.

BOOK SIX — THE LAST

In which there is one left

I. The Empty House

The house with the face above the door burned in a night, and no one came to put it out, and in the morning the face had cracked in two and the mouth was gone.

The people outside the village did not know His name. Some of them wore it on their shirts because a merchant had printed it as a joke, and the joke was that it meant nothing.

He did not rebuild. He did not send for anyone. He walked through the ash once, in the morning, and looked at the cracked face for a while, and then He went up the hill.

II. The Hill

He sat on the wall where ChooChooCReW had been drawing water. The wall is low and the stone is warm in the afternoon and it is a good place to sit if you have nowhere to be.

The donkey came up the hill, because it had nowhere else to be either, and stood beside Him, and did not bray.

And there was none beside Him.

I did not see this. I was already beyond the boundary, with the book and the pen and the scar on my hand. I have written it from what I know of Him, which is more than I have admitted and less than I claimed.

If I am wrong about the donkey, I would like to be wrong about the donkey.

BOOK SEVEN — THE SCRIBE'S NOTE

Which is not scripture, and should have been at the front

I have written that He called me on the mountain. He did not. The mountain was empty, and I was cold, and I came down and told them otherwise.

I have written that fire came down and struck me for my love. No fire came down. I burned the house myself, and I have kept the scar as evidence, and the evidence is a lie.

I have written myself into the company of the faithful, in the lists, at the end of the book. There was no company. There was Him, and there was a man at a well, and there was a donkey, and there was me, and I was the one who was not called.

The book is mine. He asked me to keep the record, and this is the record: a man who was not called, writing a god who did not need him, for a church that was never there.

I have kept the moustache in the account, because it is the best thing about Him, and because I drew it from the stones before I ever saw His face, and I would like it noted that I got it right.

If thou hast read this far, thou art the congregation. There is one of thee. That is more than He had.
